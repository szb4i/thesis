\chapter{Introduction}\label{chapter:introduction}
In this chapter, the theoretical background of the thesis is written. First, the basics of X-ray radiography is introduced, then Digital Subtraction Angiography (\gls{dsa}) is described briefly and it is shown how Digital Variance Angiography (\gls{dva}) differs from the prior approach. Next, it is shown how intensity transformations can be utilized to enhance crucial image features. Later, spatial and frequency domain filtering techniques are presented. In the end of the chapter, further methods are discussed which do not fall into the previously mentioned categories.

\section{X-ray Radiography}
X-ray radiography is a powerful tool used in medical imaging. It is a non-invasive imaging technique that uses a beam of X-rays to create images of the inside of the body relatively fast. X-ray is a form of electromagnetic radiation which has a wavelength ranging from about $10^{-8}\:m$ to $ 10^{-12}\:m$ (from $3\:\cdot\:10^{16}\:Hz$ to $3\:\cdot\:10^{19}\:Hz$) and energy between 20 - 120 $keV$ \cite{rontgen1896new}. 

There are several interactions possible when X-rays interact with matter \cite{seibert2005x}. Here we only discuss the effects of photoelectric absorption and Compton scattering to beam attenuation as these are the most dominant factors in the energy range of medical imaging (Figure \ref{fig:cross-section}).

\begin{figure}[htpb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{figures/intro/cross-section.JPG}
  \caption{Various X-ray cross sections as function of energy for Calcium} \label{fig:cross-section}
\end{figure}

Assuming an inhomogeneous sample and a monoenergetic beam, Lambert-Beer's law can be expressed as:
\begin{equation}
    I(d)=I_0\cdot\exp(-\int_{0}^{d}\mu(s)\cdot d(s))
    \label{eq:labmert-beer}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    p(d)=-\ln(\frac{I(d)}{I_0})=\int_{0}^{d}\mu(s)\cdot ds
    \label{eq:line-integral}
\end{equation}


\emph{p(d)} is called the line integral. \emph{I(d)} is the intensity in distance \emph{d} behind the initial intensity \emph{I(0)}. \emph{\textmu (s)} is the linear attenuation coefficient, which is composed by cross sections of different interactions. In X-ray radiography, the beam is leaving the X-ray tube with the initial intensity \emph{I(0)}, then interacts with tissues of various mass attenuation coefficients (\emph{\textmu (s)}).  As it reaches the detector, the initial intensity is reduced to \emph{I(d)}.

Based on equation \ref{eq:line-integral}, it can be explained that materials with higher \emph{\textmu} values are more visible on X-ray images. In the human body, these materials with high \emph{\textmu} are mostly bones. Figure \ref{fig:attenuation-for-bone-and-tissue} compares the attenuation of bones and soft tissue. In the diagnostic energy range, bone contributes the most significantly to the total attenuation, therefore it is the most visible tissue on the final image.

\begin{figure}[htpb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{figures/intro/attenuation-for-bone-and-tissue.png}
  \caption{The mass attenuation coefficient ($\mu_{m}=\mu / \rho_{m}$ where $\rho_{m}$ is the mass density) as a function of photon energy for bone and soft tissue within the diagnostic energy ranges (30–150 $keV$) \cite{kim2013optimal}} \label{fig:attenuation-for-bone-and-tissue}
\end{figure}

As a conclusion, visualizing vessel structure is not possible using traditional X-ray radiography. To solve this issue, angiography has been evolved.

\section{Angiography}
In Angiography, a contrast agent is injected into the blood vessel. The most popular types of these contrast agents are iodine-based contrast media. As Figure \ref{fig:attenuation-for-several-materials} shows, the mass attenuation coefficient of iodine is relatively high compared to the tissues in the human body. Utilizing this attribute of iodine, visualization of vessels is possible for that short time when the contrast agent expands in the human body right after injection.

\begin{figure}[htpb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{figures/intro/attenuation-for-several-materials.png}
  \caption{The mass attenuation coefficient as a function of photon energy for several materials encountered in diagnostic X-ray imaging \cite{seibert2005x}} \label{fig:attenuation-for-several-materials}
\end{figure}

There are different methods to acquire the final image containing blood vessels only (angiogram). First, the more popular technique, Digital Subtraction Angiography (\gls{dsa}) is discussed.

\subsection{Digital Subtraction Angiography} \label{subsection:dsa}
As digitization of images was introduced and manipulation of digitized information became widely used in the 1970s, Digital Subtraction Angiography (\gls{dsa}) became practical \cite{jeans1990development}. In this method, an image is taken before injecting the contrast agent into the blood vessel. Later, this pre-contrast image is being subtracted from the images acquired at different times during the movement of the contrast agent so that only structures filled with  the agent are visualized and irrelevant anatomic features disappear \cite{levin1984digital}. Such procedure can be seen on Figure \ref{fig:/angio-frames}.

\begin{figure}[htpb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{figures/intro/angio-frames.png}
  \caption{Frames captured at different times and the \gls{dsa} image. To remove static anatomical features, the first frame is subtracted from the subsequent frames. The final \gls{dsa} image is obtained by subtracting the first frame from the mean of all frames.} \label{fig:/angio-frames}
\end{figure}

\gls{dsa} is a potent imaging technique that can give precise details about the structure and operation of the vascular system. It can be used to identify a variety of vascular conditions, including aneurysms \cite{chappell2003comparison}, peripheral artery disease \cite{fleischmann2006ct}  and coronary artery disease \cite{ambrose1988angiographic}.

Unfortunately, the procedure has some drawbacks. The injection of contrast material is one of the most common risks associated with \gls{dsa}. While the material is generally safe, allergic reactions or other adverse reactions are possible \cite{tramer2006pharmacological}. Another concern is the radiation exposure induced by X-rays, which should always be minimized in medical applications \cite{lange2006randomized}. Digital variance angiography is a viable solution to these issues.


\subsection{Digital Variance Angiography} \label{subsection:dva}

The key benefit of Digital variance angiography (\gls{dva}) is the new motion-based contrast scheme it introduces, which enables the visualization of structures that are not visible in a traditional X-ray image. Instead of capturing a single fully exposed image during the procedure, several underexposed images are taken. By calculating the variance and the mean for every pixel, two types of images can be obtained. The expected value of the X-ray attenuation represents the static image, which contains the same type of information as the conventional X-ray image. On the other hand,  the variance of the X-ray attenuation represents a new type of image, named kinetic image, reflecting internal motions \cite{szigeti2014motion}.

Figure \ref{fig:clock-kinetic} introduces the previously mentioned static and kinetic images on an alarm clock. The static image (top) shows the electronic circuitry and the mechanics of the clock. In the kinetic image of the same part of the clock (bottom) the still parts disappear, and only moving parts are visualized.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.45\textwidth]{figures/intro/clock-kinetic.png}
  \caption{X-ray image of a clock. Top: static image. Bottom: kinetic image \cite{szigeti2014motion}} \label{fig:clock-kinetic}
\end{figure}

Angiography is a promising area for the application of the novel method. As the new information is based on the variance of measured intensities, it is called Digital variance angiography (\gls{dva}). Patient studies showed that \gls{dva} can be  can be used as a substitute for \gls{dsa}. Side-by-side comparison is shown on Figure \ref{fig:dsa-vs-dva}. It is noticeable that \gls{dva} significantly reduces background noise and effectively enhances the vascular contour.  The major advantage of \gls{dva} could be the significant dose reduction. Furthermore, \gls{dva} might help the widespread use of $CO_2$ as a safer contrast agent in clinical practice \cite{orias2019digital}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.65\textwidth]{figures/intro/dsa-vs-dva.png}
  \caption{Upper row: \gls{dsa}. Lower row: \gls{dva}. Postprocessing techniques were applied on every image \cite{orias2019digital}} \label{fig:dsa-vs-dva}
\end{figure}

Similar to \gls{dsa}, \gls{dva} also requires further postprocessing in order to improve the visibility of the vessel region. The next section introduces several techniques that might be helpful.

\section{Image Processing}

An image can be thought of as a two-dimensional function, $f (x, y)$ where $x$ and $y$ are spatial coordinates. The intensity of the image is defined as the amplitude of $f$ at any given pair of coordinates $(x, y)$. We refer to the image as a digital image when $x$, $y$ and the intensity values of $f$ are all finite, discrete quantities. Processing digital images using a computer is referred to as the field of digital image processing \cite{gonzalez2018digital}.

\subsection{Intensity Transformations}\label{subsection:intensity-transformations}

The following is the general formula for intensity transformations:

\begin{equation}
    g(x,y)=T[f(x,y)]
    \label{eq:intensity-transform}
\end{equation}

where $f(x,y)$ is the input image, $g(x,y)$ is the output image and $T$ is an operator on $f$ defined over a neighborhood of point $(x,y)$. When a neighborhood is of size 1x1, $g$ is determined solely by the value of $f$ at a single point $(x,y)$ and $T$ can be reduced to the form:

\begin{equation}
    s=T(r)
    \label{eq:intensity-transform-simplified}
\end{equation}

where $s$ and $r$ denote the intensity after and before the intensity transformation respectively.  The simplest intensity transformations are contrast stretching and thresholding which can be seen on Figure \ref{fig:/stretching-and-thresholding}. By utilizing these methods, one can achieve higher contrast than the original. Also, binary images can be created if $T$ is a thresholding function.

\begin{figure}[htpb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{figures/intro/stretching-and-thresholding.png}
  \caption{Intensity stretching and thresholding \cite{gonzalez2018digital}} \label{fig:/stretching-and-thresholding}
\end{figure}

If the majority of an image's details are concentrated in a dark, narrow region of the histogram, using power law transformation can significantly improve visibility. These transformations can be expressed as the following:

\begin{equation}
    s=r^{\gamma}
    \label{eq:power-law}
\end{equation}

where $\gamma$ is a positive constant which can be tuned for the desired needs as shown on Figure \ref{fig:/power-law}.

\begin{figure}[htpb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{figures/intro/power-law.png}
  \caption{Power law transformations with various $\gamma$ values (Code Snippet \ref{code:power-law}) \cite{tree-img}}
  \label{fig:/power-law}
\end{figure}


Another effective way to enhance the global contrast of images is applying histogram equalization. The basic idea is to distribute intensities more evenly when the image is represented by a narrow range of intensity values. Figure \ref{fig:/unequalized_hist} shows an image with its histogram.  The histogram is constrained to a narrow region which results poor contrast.

\begin{figure}[htpb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{figures/intro/unequalized_hist.png}
  \caption{Original image and its histogram (Code Snippet \ref{code:histogram}) \cite{capper2006unequalized} \cite{bradski2000opencv} }
  \label{fig:/unequalized_hist}
\end{figure}

Ideally, the cumulative distribution function  \gls{cdf} is linearized across the value range. During histogram equalization, the values from the original narrow range are being mapped to the full range. Here, only the output is shown, the step-by-step algorithm can be found in the references \cite{gonzalez2018digital}.

\begin{figure}[htpb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{figures/intro/equalized_hist.png}
  \caption{Equalized image and its histogram \cite{bradski2000opencv}}
  \label{fig:/equalized_hist}
\end{figure}

However, when the approach is applied globally, the final output might not be as desired. As it can be seen on Figure \ref{fig:/global-hist-eq}, the dark background's fine details are emphasized, but the characteristics of the face are lost in the foreground. One solution can be applying histogram equalization locally in order to preserve previously visible features while exposing predominantly hidden ones.

\begin{figure}[htpb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{figures/intro/global-hist-eq.png}
  \caption{Side effects of global histogram equalization \cite{bradski2000opencv}}
  \label{fig:/global-hist-eq}
\end{figure}

The suggested approach is called \gls{clahe} (Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization) \cite{pizer1987adaptive}. Instead of equalizing the histogram globally, the image is partitioned into equal-sized regions known as tiles, each of which is subjected to individual histogram equalization as previously demonstrated. By simply doing so, noise might be amplified in near-constant regions. To prevent this, before applying histogram equalization, pixels that are above the given contrast limit in any histogram bin are clipped and evenly distributed to other bins. Finally, tile boundary artefacts are eliminated using bilinear interpolation. The outcome is shown in Figure \ref{fig:/intro-clahe}. While histogram equalization enhanced successfully the visibility of the dark background, there was also a slight improvement in the visibility of the face in the foreground.

\begin{figure}[htpb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{figures/intro/clahe.png}
  \caption{\gls{clahe} with clipLimit=2.0 and tileGridSize=(8,8) (Code Snippet \ref{code:clahe}) \cite{bradski2000opencv}}
  \label{fig:/intro-clahe}
\end{figure}

As it was covered in this subsection, intensity transformations provide a useful toolkit when contrast enhancement is required. However, if image smoothing or sharpening is desired, spatial filtering must be used.


\subsection{Filtering in Spatial Domain} \label{subsection:introduction:filtering-in-spatial-domain}

Spatial ﬁltering modiﬁes an image by replacing the value of each pixel by a function of the values of the pixel and its neighbors \cite{gonzalez2018digital}. The general formula for linear spatial filtering can be written in these forms:

\begin{equation}
	g(x,y)=\sum_{s=-a}^{a}\sum_{t=-b}^{b}w(s,t)f(x+s,y+t)
	\label{eq:correlation}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
	g(x,y)=\sum_{s=-a}^{a}\sum_{t=-b}^{b}w(s,t)f(x-s,y-t)
	\label{eq:convolution}
\end{equation}

where $g(x,y)$ is the output pixel, $w$ is the kernel, $f(x,y)$ is a pixel of the original image, $s$ is the index iterating in the vertical direction within distance $2a$ and $t$ is the index iterating in the horizontal direction within distance $2b$. Here, odd-sized, symmetric kernels are discussed, mostly 3x3. Figure \ref{fig:/convolution} illustrates the process.

\begin{figure}[htpb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{figures/intro/convolution.png}
  \caption{The mechanics of linear spatial ﬁltering using a 3 × 3 kernel \cite{basavarajaiah2019convolution}}
  \label{fig:/convolution}
\end{figure}

The method consists of moving the kernel's center over an image and computing the product sum at each position. Essentially, Equation \ref{eq:correlation} is a correlation between the kernel and the image. In Equation \ref{eq:convolution}, which is known as convolution, minus signs are used in the arguments of $f$. However, convolution and correlation provide the same result when the kernel is symmetric. In this thesis, convolution, Equation \ref{eq:convolution}, is preferred and used subsequently.

Many times, one wishes to blur or sharpen an image. First, it is covered how the prior can be obtained. The most convenient kernel for this purpose is a lowpass gaussian kernel:

\begin{equation}
	w(s,t)=e^{-\frac{s^{2}+t^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}
	\label{eq:lowpass-gaussian-spatial}
\end{equation}

where $\sigma$ is the standard deviation.  As the numerator of the exponent represents the distance from the center of the kernel, the expression can be simplified to the following form:

\begin{equation}
	w(r)=Ke^{-\frac{r^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}
	\label{eq:lowpass-gaussian-spatial-r}
\end{equation}

where $r$ is the distance from the center of the kernel. In image processing, filter kernels are used in their discrete forms because images contain finite number of non-zero-sized pixels. Therefore, a 3x3 lowpass gaussian kernel with $\sigma=1$ is used as it can be seen on Figure \ref{fig:/lowpass-gaussian-spatial-discrete}.

\begin{figure}[htpb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{figures/intro/lowpass-gaussian.png}
  \caption{Normalized 3x3 gaussian kernel \cite{gonzalez2018digital}}
  \label{fig:/lowpass-gaussian-spatial-discrete}
\end{figure}

Figure \ref{fig:/lowpass-gaussian-example} shows the effects of convolving an image with lowpass gaussian kernels. As it can be seen, bluring is noticeable on the branches of the tree even when the 3x3 kernel is used. The blurring becomes more obvious if the kernel size is extended to 7x7 because the kernel covers more nearby pixels.

\begin{figure}[htpb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{figures/intro/lowpass-gaussian-example.png}
  \caption{Image convolved with lowpass gaussian kernels (Code Snippet \ref{code:gaussian-low-pass}) \cite{tree-img}}
  \label{fig:/lowpass-gaussian-example}
\end{figure}

As it was mentioned earlier, another common image manipulation technique is sharpening. After sharpening, it is expected that sharp visual details, such as edges, become more prominent. The filters used for such purposes are based on first and second order derivatives. First,  for the sake of simplification, the derivative of a 1D function is discussed which can be written as the following:

\begin{equation}
	\frac{df}{dx} = f(x+1) -f(x) 
	\label{eq:1st-derivative-1d}
\end{equation}

By expanding Equation \ref{eq:1st-derivative-1d} to 2D, the following form can be obtained:

\begin{equation}
	\nabla f =\left[\begin{array}{c}
	\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x}\\
	\\
	\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial y} \\
	\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}{c}
	g_x\\
	g_y \\
	\end{array}\right]
	\label{eq:1st-derivative-2d}
\end{equation}

where $\nabla f$ is the gradient of the image which is a 2D vector pointing in the direction of the greatest rate of change in $(x, y)$. As noted earlier, discrete implementation of kernel functions is necessary for image processing. For simplification, to represent the pixel location in a specific region, the indexing shown in Figure \ref{fig:kernel-indexing} is used.

A possible implementation for a pair of 3x3 discretized gradient kernels is the following:

\begin{equation}
	g_x = \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x}=(z_7+2z_8+z_9)-(z_1+2z_2+z_3)  
	\label{eq:1st-derivative-2d-x}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
	g_y = \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial y}=(z_3+2z_6+z_9)-(z_1+2z_4+z_7)  
	\label{eq:1st-derivative-2d-y}
\end{equation}

These kernels are known as the Sobel operators \cite{kanopoulos1988design}. The kernels displayed on Figure \ref{fig:sobel-kernels} approximate the partial derivative in $x$ and $y$ direction, respectively.

\begin{figure}[h]

When the 1st order derivative is needed, the original image has to be convolved with the two Sobel kernels individually, resulting $g_x$ and $g_y$. From these values, a magnitude image can be calculated the following way:

\begin{equation}
	M(x,y)=\sqrt[]{g_x^{2}+g_y^{2}}
	\label{eq:sobel-magnitued}
\end{equation}

Each steps of this process can been seen on Figure \ref{fig:sobel-tree}. It is clear that whereas $g_y$ emphasizes vertical features, $g_x$ does so for horizontal ones.

\begin{figure}[htpb]

  \caption{Using Sobel kernels (Code Snippet \ref{code:sobel-magnitude}) \cite{tree-img}}
  \label{fig:sobel-tree}
\end{figure}

In addition to the 1st gradient of an image, the 2nd gradient might also be an effective tool when edge enhancement is desired. It can be written as the following:

\begin{equation}
	\nabla ^{2} f = \frac{\partial ^{2} f}{\partial x^{2}} + \frac{\partial ^{2} f}{\partial y^{2}}
	\label{eq:12nd-derivative}
\end{equation}

where $\nabla ^{2} f$ is the Laplacian of the image. A possible discretized implementation is shown on Figure \ref{fig:laplacian-kernel}.

\begin{figure}[htpb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.25\textwidth]{figures/intro/laplacian-kernel.png}
  \caption{Laplacian kernel \cite{gonzalez2018digital}}
  \label{fig:laplacian-kernel}
\end{figure}

The output (center image on Figure \ref{eq:laplacian-output}) contains a significant number of pixels with negative values after convolution with the previously used tree image. Setting values below zero to zero is one way to deal with this problem. It can be noticed that the Laplacian filter returns strong response at locations of intensity discontinuities. By adding the result to the original image, the sharpness can be significantly improved as it is shown on the right image of Figure \ref{eq:laplacian-output}. The previously shown algorithm is the implementation of the following equation:

\begin{equation}
	g(x,y)=f(x,y)+c[\nabla ^{2} f ]
	\label{eq:laplacian-output}
\end{equation}

where the sign of $c$ depends on the implementation of the Laplacian kernel. If the center element is negative $c$ needs to be negative too. By changing the magnitude of $c$, the weight of the sharpening can be controlled.

\begin{figure}[htpb]
  \centering

  \caption{Convolving with Laplacian kernel. $c$ was set to 0.5 (Code Snippet \ref{code:laplacian-filtered}) \cite{tree-img}}

Both 1st and 2nd gradient is useful when image sharpening is needed. The two, however, behave in different ways near edges as it is shown on Figure \ref{fig:1st-vs-2nd-gradient}. In constant intensity areas, both derivatives are zero. When reaching the ramp, the 1st derivative is non-zero until the end of the ramp. On the other hand, the second derivative is only non-zero at the start and the end of the ramp. The sign of the second derivative changes at the onset and end of a step or ramp.  As a conclusion, 2nd order gradient highlights fine details better while 1st order gradient enhances prominent edges more effectively.

The prior methods are general transformations. They affect the entire image globally. As a consequence, they enhance both fine details and noise. In order to reduce noise, adaptive spatial filters can be used.  Consider a 3×3 neighborhood around a single center pixel $l(x, y)$ in $I$, where $I(x, y)$ is the input image of the size W×H. The local intensity mean $\mu$ and local signal energy variation $\epsilon$ of the 3×3 neighborhood pixels were deﬁned using the following expressions:

\begin{equation}
	\mu(x,y)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=-1}^{1	}\sum_{j=-1}^{1	}I(x+i,y+j)
	\label{eq:local-mean}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
	\epsilon(x,y)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=-1}^{1	}\sum_{j=-1}^{1	}(I(x+i,y+j)-\mu(x,y))^{2}
	\label{eq:local-variance}
\end{equation}

where $N=9$ is a normalizing constant. Transforming the 3×3 neighborhood elements of the
kernel operator into a column vector, it can be described as the follwoing:

\begin{equation}
	\mathbf{x}=[x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},...,x_{9}]^{t}
	\label{eq:x-column-vector}
\end{equation}

In its vectorized form, Equation \ref{eq:local-variance} can be rewritten as:

\begin{equation}
	\epsilon(x,y)=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\mathbf{x}^{t}\mathbf{C}\mathbf{x}
	\label{eq:local-variance-vectorized}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
	\mathbf{C}=\begin{bmatrix}
		8 & -1 & -1 & -1 & -1 & -1 & -1 & -1 & -1\\
		-1 & 8 & -1 & -1 & -1 & -1 & -1 & -1 & -1\\
		-1 & -1 & 8 & -1 & -1 & -1 & -1 & -1 & -1\\
		-1 & -1 & -1 & 8 & -1 & -1 & -1 & -1 & -1\\
		-1 & -1 & -1 & -1 & 8 & -1 & -1 & -1 & -1\\
		-1 & -1 & -1 & -1 & -1 & 8 & -1 & -1 & -1\\
		-1 & -1 & -1 & -1 & -1 & -1 & 8 & -1 & -1\\
		-1 & -1 & -1 & -1 & -1 & -1 & -1 & 8 & -1\\
		-1 & -1 & -1 & -1 & -1 & -1 & -1 & -1 & 8\\
	\end{bmatrix}
	\label{eq:wl-matrix}
\end{equation}

The vectorized form shown in Equation \ref{eq:local-variance-vectorized} and \ref{eq:wl-matrix} can be useful for implementing the filter in any programming language. However, the following form of Equation \ref{eq:local-variance} expresses the power of the method better:

\begin{equation}
	\epsilon(x,y)=\frac{1}{N^{2}}[(x_{1}-x_{2})^{2}+(x_{1}-x_{3})^{2}+(x_{1}-x_{4})^{2}+...+(x_{8}-x_{9})^{2}]
	\label{eq:local-variance-algebraic}
\end{equation}

With permutation of square differences in the 3×3 kernel pixel values in Equation \ref{eq:local-variance-algebraic}, there exists 36 directive components in the response value. The recommended operator can be considered of as an adaptive operator since, unlike typical filters with constant kernel elements, it uses different kernel elements at various locations throughout the image. It is called the WL operator \cite{lin2018edge}.

Figure \ref{fig:sobel-vs-wl} shows the differences from the Sobel operator. On the WL image, there are less highlighted edges. The lines on the filed an the contour of the clouds are both ignored. Also in the tree region, edge detection is less sensible.

Consider a noisy image as in case of Figure \ref{fig:sobel-vs-wl-noisy}. Compared to Sobel kernels, the WL operator more effectively reduces noise. Therefore it is not clear which operator performs better in general. It has been demonstrated thus far that an adaptive filter may be a better option for noisy photos while an adaptive filter may miss essential information in ideal photographs. Further adaptive methods were tested during the research phase of the thesis \cite{bharodiya2019improved} \cite{tu2009coronary}, however, the same conclusion applies to all.

\subsection{Filtering in Frequency Domain} \label{subsection:introduction:filtering-in-frequency-domain}

The following description of the Fourier domain is based on Book \cite{gonzalez2018digital}.  A function $f(t)$ of a continuous variable $t$ that is periodic with a period $T$ can be expressed as the sum of sines and cosines:

\begin{equation}
	f(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(A_{k}cos(\omega_{k}t)+B_{k}cos(\omega_{k}t))
	\label{eq:sum-sin-cos}
\end{equation}

where $\omega_k=\frac{2\pi}{T}$. Utilizing Euler's formula, Equation \ref{eq:sum-sin-cos} can be transformed to:

\begin{equation}
	f(t)=\sum_{-\infty}^{\infty}c_{n}e^{j\frac{2\pi n}{T}t}
	\label{eq:sum-sin-cos-euler}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
	c_{n}=\frac{1}{T}\int_{-T/2}^{T/2}e^{-j\frac{2\pi n}{T}t}dt
	\label{eq:sum-sin-cos-euler-normalization}
\end{equation}

The Fourier transform $\mathcal{F}\{f(t)\}$ of a continuous function $f(t)$ of a continuous variable $t$ and the inverse Fourier transform $\mathcal{F}^{-1}\{F(\mu)\}$ of a continuous function $F(\mu)$ of a continuous variable $\mu$  is defined by the following equations:

\begin{equation}
	\mathcal{F}\{f(t)\}=F(\mu)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(t)e^{-j2\pi \mu t}dt
	\label{eq:fourier-transform}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
	\mathcal{F}^{-1}\{F(\mu)\}=f(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}F(\mu)e^{j2\pi \mu t}d\mu
	\label{eq:inverse-fourier-transform}
\end{equation}

The domain of the Fourier transform is called the frequency domain ($F(\mu)$), while the original domain is called the spatial domain ($f(t)$). The units of $\mu$ depend on the units of $t$. In case of image processing, $t$ represents distance in meters and therefore the units of $\mu$ are cycles/meter. Figure \ref{fig:box-fourier} shows the Fourier transform of a box function. Inverse relationship can be noticed between the width in spatial domain and the location of zeros in frequency domain. The function in frequency domain extends to infinity in both directions. Furthermore, the discontinuities of $f(t)$ at $-W/2$ and $W/2$ introduces ringing which is the result of the Gibbs phenomenon \cite{gottlieb1997gibbs}.

\begin{figure}[htpb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{figures/intro/box-fourier.png}
  \caption{
  Left: Box function of width $W$.\\\hspace{\textwidth}
   Right: Fourier transform of a box function \cite{gonzalez2018digital}.
   }
  \label{fig:box-fourier}
\end{figure}

So far, continuous functions have been observed. However, in image processing one has to deal with discrete functions as pixels have fixed size. Consider a continuous function $f(t)$ and a sampling interval $\triangle T$. One way to model sampling is to multiply $f(t)$ by a sampling function $\triangle T$ units apart. The sampled function can be written as the following:

\begin{equation}
	\tilde{f}(t)=f(t)s_{\triangle T}(t)=\sum_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(t)\delta (t-n\triangle T)
	\label{eq:fourier-transform-sampled}
\end{equation}

where $s_{\triangle T}(t)$ is an impulse train of infinitely many Dirac delta functions (unit impulses) $\triangle T$ units apart. Each component of this summation is an impulse weighted by $f(t)$ which can be expressed the way it is shown in Equation \ref{eq:fourier-transform-sampled-component}. Figure \ref{fig:sampled-function} provides an illustration of sampling.

\begin{equation}
	f_{k}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(t)\delta(t-k\triangle T)dt=f(k\triangle T)
	\label{eq:fourier-transform-sampled-component}
\end{equation}

\begin{figure}[htpb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{figures/intro/sampled-function.png}
  \caption{
	Top: Continuous function.\\\hspace{\textwidth}
   Center: Train of impulses.\\\hspace{\textwidth}
	Bottom: Sampled values \cite{gonzalez2018digital}.
   }
  \label{fig:sampled-function}
\end{figure}

To determine the sufficient length of $\triangle T$, one has to know the concept of Nyquist rate \cite{landau1967sampling}. Consider a band-limited function displayed on Figure \ref{fig:band-limited}.

\begin{figure}[htpb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{figures/intro/band-limited.png}
  \caption{
	Top: Continuous function.\\\hspace{\textwidth}
	Bottom: Sampled values \cite{gonzalez2018digital}.
   }
  \label{fig:band-limited}
\end{figure}

Sufficient separation of a periodic sequence is possible if the following condition apply:

\begin{equation}
	\frac{1}{\triangle T}>2\mu_{max}
	\label{eq:nyquist}
\end{equation}

It is called the sampling theorem and $\mu_{max}=\frac{1}{2\triangle T}$ is the Nyquist rate which is the minimum frequency required for sufficient recovery. If the above condition is not fulfilled different signals may become indistinguishable. This artefact is known as aliasing.

The previously mentioned theorems can be extended to two dimension space which can be investigated further in Book \cite{gonzalez2018digital}.  Here, only the 2D discrete Fourier transform and its inverse are written respectively as follows:

\begin{equation}
	F(u,v)=\sum_{x=0}^{M-1}\sum_{y=0}^{N-1}f(x,y)e^{-j2\pi (ux/M + vy/N)}
	\label{eq:2d-fourier}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
	f(x,y)=\frac{1}{MN}\sum_{u=0}^{M-1}\sum_{v=0}^{N-1}F(u,v)e^{j2\pi (ux/M + vy/N)}
	\label{eq:2d-fourier-inverse}
\end{equation}

where $f(x, y)$ is a digital image of size $M\times N$. When writing a Python algorithm, all the previously mentioned theory is encapsulated by pre-written functions of many libraries. Appendix \ref{appendix:a} towards the end of the thesis contains a list of all the used dependencies. Appendix \ref{appendix:b} includes code snippets used in the thesis.

Figure \ref{fig:tree-fourier} shows the output of the discrete Fourier transform of an example image. The low frequency components are at the center of the frequency domain while the high frequency components are further away in the radial direction.

Convolution in the frequency domain can be done easily. One needs to apply the convolution theorem which can be expressed as:

\begin{equation}
	(f \circledast h)(t) \Leftrightarrow (H \cdot F)(\mu)
	\label{eq:convolution-theorem}
\end{equation}

where $f$ is the image in spatial domain, $F$ is the image in frequency domain, $h$ is the filter in spatial domain and $H$ is the filter in frequency domain. The $ \circledast$ operator on the left means convolution which was introduced earlier with Equation \ref{eq:convolution}.  As a result, convolution simplifies to a multiplication in frequency domain instead of sliding over the whole image in spatial domain. However, the cost of the discrete Fourier transformation and the inverse discrete Fourier transformation remains a consideration in terms of computational efficiency.  Fortunately, the Fast Fourier Transform (\gls{fft}) technique \cite{brigham1988fast} is quite effective. It reduces the complexity of computing the discrete Fourier transform from $O(N^{2})$ to $O(N \log N)$ where $N$ is the data size. The Python libraries used in the thesis all utilize the power of \gls{fft}.

To create a low-pass filter in frequency domain to highlight edges only, the most plausible solution might be ignoring completely frequency components above a certain distance from the center in frequency space. It is called the ideal low-pass filter (\gls{ilpf}) and it can be expressed as follows:

\begin{equation}
  H(u,v) =
    \begin{cases}
      1 & \text{if $D(u,v) \le D_{0}$}\\
      0 & \text{if $D(u,v) > D_{0}$}\\
    \end{cases}       
    \label{eq:ilpf}
\end{equation}

where $D(u,v)$ is the distance from the center of the frequency domain and $D_{0}$ is a positive constant. The details of implementation is shown on Figure \ref{code:ilpf}.x` Figure \ref{fig:tree-ilpf} displays the filter in use. 

\begin{figure}[htpb]
  \centering

  \caption{
	Left: Original image \cite{tree-img} \\\hspace{\textwidth}
	Center: \gls{ilpf} filter in frequency domain  \\\hspace{\textwidth}
	Right: Filtered image
   }
  \label{fig:tree-ilpf}
\end{figure}

Unfortunately, there is a substantial ringing artefact on the output image (right image on Figure \ref{fig:tree-ilpf}). To better understand the issue, it is necessary to observe the spatial domain kernel of the \gls{ilpf} obtained by calculating the inverse discrete Fourier transform of \gls{ilpf} in frequency domain. An illustration can be seen on Figure \ref{fig:ilpf-ringing}.

\begin{figure}[htpb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{figures/intro/ilpf-ringing.png}
  \caption{
	Left: \gls{ilpf} in frequency domain \\\hspace{\textwidth}
	Center: Corresponding spatial domain kernel  \\\hspace{\textwidth}
	Right: Intensity proﬁle of a horizontal line through the center \cite{gonzalez2018digital}
   }
  \label{fig:ilpf-ringing}
\end{figure}

As a reminder, filtering in the spatial domain is accomplished by convolving the center function in Figure \ref{fig:ilpf-ringing} with the image.  Ringing is primarily caused by the outer part of this spatial function, while blurring is mainly done by the center. The \gls{ilpf} has to be replaced by a function that remains unchanged after an inverse Fourier transform in order to leave the undesirable ringing artefact. An adequate function is Gaussian which can be expressed as follows:

\begin{equation}
	H(u, v)= e^{-D^{2}(u,v)/2\sigma ^{2}}
	\label{eq:glpf}
\end{equation}

where $D(u,v)$ is the distance from the center of the frequency domain and $\sigma$ is a measure of spread about the center. Replacing \gls{ilpf} with Gaussian low-pass filter (\gls{glpf}), the result is shown on Figure \ref{fig:tree-glpf}. As it can be noticed, the unwanted ringing artefact is eliminated while the blurring is accomplished successfully.

   }
  \label{fig:tree-glpf}
\end{figure}

Next, high-pass filtering is discussed. In order to create a high-pass filter, the previously introduced \gls{glpf} can be reused. The Gaussian high-pass filter (\gls{ghpf}) can be expressed as follows:

\begin{equation}
	H(u, v)= 1- e^{-D^{2}(u,v)/2\sigma ^{2}}
	\label{eq:ghpf}
\end{equation}

The filtered image is displayed on the 3rd image of Figure \ref{fig:tree-ghpf}. It can be noticed that in the background of the filtered image the smooth intensity variations in the clouds are ignored and only the sharp changes are highlighted near the field and the tree. On the other hand, because the DC term (the center of the frequency domain) is filtered out, the average intensity of the image is decreased. If the DC term is required, the computation of the inverse Fourier transform can be modified as follows:

\begin{equation}
	g(x,y)=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\{(1+H_{hp})F(u,v)\}
	\label{eq:ghpf-plus-dc}
\end{equation}

where $H_{hp}$ is $H$ from Equation \ref{eq:ghpf}. The result is shown on the 4th image of Figure \ref{fig:tree-ghpf}.

Knowing \gls{glpf} and \gls{ghpf}, one can try creating a band-pass filter. Consider two Gaussians with different $\sigma$ values. The difference of the two leaves passage only for a narrow band in the frequency domain. It can be expressed as:

\begin{equation}
	H(u,v)=e^{-D^{2}(u,v)/2\sigma_{2}^{2}}-e^{-D^{2}(u,v)/2\sigma_{1}^{2}}
	\label{eq:gauss-diff}
\end{equation}

where $\sigma_{2} > \sigma_{1}$. Figure \ref{fig:tree-bandpass-gauss-diff} shows the filter in use. The application of a band-pass filter can be effective when highlighting structure with a specific width is required - e.g. víessel structure. Furthermore, it suppresses high frequency noise.

\begin{equation}
	H(u,v)=1-e^{-\frac{(D(u,v)-C_{0})^{2}}{W^{2}}}
	\label{eq:gauss-alternative}
\end{equation}

where $C_{0}$ is the center of the band and $W$ is the width of the band. Application is shown on Figure \ref{fig:tree-bandpass-gauss-alternative}. Comparing the middle images of Figure \ref{fig:tree-bandpass-gauss-alternative} and \ref{fig:tree-bandpass-gauss-diff}, it can be noticed that the prior provides a thinner band. However, by widening $W$, the earlier shown ringing artefact (see Figure \ref{fig:tree-ilpf}) is starting to appeal.

As it has been demonstrated in this chapter, frequency domain filtering is a crucial technique along with spatial domain filtering. When one needs to highlight visual elements with particular frequencies, frequency domain filtering is a more obvious choice. In case of large images, filtering in frequency domain performs computationally far more effectively than sliding across the entire image in spatial domain. \subsection{Enhancement with Principal Component Analysis}\label{subsection:introduction:pca}

Principal Component Analysis (\gls{pca}) is regarded by many as a highly prevalent dimension reduction technique. In order to raise comprehensibility of the method, it is practical to look into the process within a two-dimensional context \cite{gagneur2002data}.

The goal is to merge the $x_1$ and $x_2$ attributes of different observations into a single score. From a geometric perspective, one has to identify a line that is positioned as closely as possible to the given data points. The closeness is determined by minimizing the sum of squared Euclidean distances.

\begin{figure}[htpb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{figures/intro/pca2d.png}
  \caption{
		$x_1$ and $x_2$ attributes of different observations
   }
  \label{fig:pca-2d}
\end{figure}

In this analysis, it is assumed that all variables are centered, causing the line passing through the origin. Let $w$ be a unit direction vector denoting the line. The closest point on the line to the observation vector $x_i$ is its orthogonal projection $p(x_i)$ which is equal to the scalar product of the direction vector and the observation vector, times the direction vector:

\begin{equation}
	p(x_i)=(w^{T}x_i)w
	\label{eq:orthogonal-projection}
\end{equation}

Hence, the optimal line that corresponds closest with all the given data points can be determined by solving the following equation:

\begin{equation}
	\begin{split}
		\min_{w}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|x_{i}-(w^{T}x_{i})w|^{2} \\
		(|w|=1)
		\label{eq:minimize-w}
	\end{split}
\end{equation}

This optimal direction vector $w$ is referred as the first principal component (\gls{pc1}) of the data.

The task can also be perceived as a maximisation problem. In this case, it may be derived from Pythagoras' theorem that:

\begin{equation}
	|x_{i}|^{2}=|p(x_{i})|^{2}+|x_{i}-p(x_{i})|^{2}
	\label{eq:max-w-pythagoras}
\end{equation}

Across the whole dataset, the sum of $|x_{i}|$ remains constant regardless of the specific selection of the directional vector $w$. Hence, the minimization issue represented by Equation \ref{eq:minimize-w} can be considered as identical to:


\begin{equation}
	\begin{split}
		\max_{w}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|p(x_{i})|^{2} \\
		(|w|=1)
		\label{eq:max-w}
	\end{split}	
\end{equation}

The data has been centered, resulting in the origin being represented by the mean of the dataset. Therefore, the sum of squared norms can be expressed as:

\begin{equation}
	\sum_{i=1}^{n}|p(x_{i})|^{2}=n\text{Var}(p(x))
	\label{eq:pca-var}
\end{equation}

Thus, it may be claimed that \gls{pc1} is maximising the variance of the data.

% In a multi dimensional scenario, involving $p$ variables and $n$ observations, the objective is to identify the $q$-dimensional plane that presents the closest proximity to the data, as measured by the sums of squared Euclidean distances (or according to Equation \ref{eq:pca-var},  the  plane that maximizes the variance of the projected data.).
%Moreover, it can be shown that the principal components correspond to the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix of the data.

In a multi-dimensional context, \gls{pca} can be used to transform the data into an alternative coordinate system. This transformation is designed to ensure that the highest variance, as determined by the orthogonal projection $p(x_i)$, is aligned with the first coordinate (identified as \gls{pc1}). Subsequently, the second highest variance is aligned with the second coordinate (identified as \gls{pc2}), and so forth.

The current thesis does not contain additional implementation-specific details. Please refer to the Bishop's book \cite{bishop2006pattern}  for further clarification and supporting evidence.

In the field of data analysis, \gls{pca} is a frequently used technique to reduce the dimensionality of the data.  This is achieved by projecting each data point onto a limited number of principle components, hence obtaining lower-dimensional data while retaining a significant portion of the variation of original data. The utilisation of dimensionality reduction in \gls{dva} will be covered in Chapter \ref{section:pca}.
